## Fact check

The sources for this topic are automotive engineering media, engine builders and forums, not institutions. Factory figures were confirmed in more than one source. The sources were collected on 23 November 2026; check them again before quoting them.

| Claim in the script | What was found | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| CR-V 2.4 litre: 160 horsepower | K24A1 (CR-V 2002 to 2006): 2,354 cc, 87 mm bore, 99 mm stroke, 160 hp at 6,000 rpm, 6,500 rpm redline | [1][2][3] |
| "runs out of breath at 6,500 rpm", an economy head | the economy K24 head has VTEC on the intake side only, and runs on 12 valves below 2,200 rpm; redline 6,500 | [1][4][6] |
| The Type R head has VTEC on both camshafts | the performance K20 head has VTEC on the intake and exhaust sides, and dual valve springs for 8,600 rpm | [5][6] |
| The nickname "Frankenstein" | a K24 block with a K20 head is commonly called "Frankenstein" or "K20/K24 Frankenstein" | [7] |
| Cast pistons crack, rods bend | stock K-series rods are reported to bend at around 300 hp; cast pistons fail at the ring land under detonation; 400 hp is still common on stock parts | [8][9][20] |
| Steel studs clamp the head | ARP L-19 studs are used so that the clamping force on the head is enough above 30 psi | [10] |
| Unsupported cylinder walls; iron sleeves | an open-deck block does not support the cylinders well at high power; ductile iron sleeves are fitted after the block is machined | [10][11][12][18] |
| Valves float at high revs | valve float: the valve no longer follows the cam, with the risk that it touches the piston; stiffer springs prevent it | [13] |
| Inconel exhaust valves | high-nickel alloys (Inconel) are used for the exhaust valves of turbo engines because of the heat | [14][15] |
| Compressed air is hot and causes knock; the intercooler | a turbo raises the intake air temperature, which can trigger detonation; an intercooler cools it | [16][17] |
| Ethanol | the example engine runs on E85 with an air-to-air intercooler | [10][18] |
| More than 1,000 hp at almost 10,000 rpm; more than 2 bar | 4 Piston Racing K24: 1,038 hp at 9,760 rpm with a little over 30 psi of boost (about 2.1 to 2.3 bar), a 72 mm turbo | [10][18][19] |
| The factory crankshaft | the OEM 99 mm K24 crankshaft is kept; almost every other part is replaced | [10] |
| Six times | 1,038 / 160 = 6.5 | calculation |

**Facts in the reference video were treated as leads, not sources.** Three things in this script differ from its reference video because of the check:

- Stock power: the script uses 160 hp, the figure for the K24A1 of the second-generation CR-V, which belongs to the same family as the block the engine builders use.
- The documented 1,038 hp engine uses a K24 block and head from an Acura TSX, not from a CR-V. That is why the script says "an engine of the same series" and not "the CR-V engine itself".
- Fuel: the documented engine runs on ethanol (E85), so the script says ethanol.

## Deliberate simplifications

- The cylinder head of the example engine is a K24 TSX head (VTEC on both camshafts, the same kind as the performance K20 head). The script says Civic Type R head because that is the usual "Frankenstein" recipe.
- "More than two bar" is boost pressure, not the pressure inside the cylinder.
- The limits (300 hp, 9,000 rpm) are ranges from workshop experience, not hard limits.
- The figure of 160 hp is the specification for the US and Japanese markets; the Indonesian variant has not been checked.
- The engine is drawn at about 7.5 times real size, in a diagram-like style. The fastest speed on screen is 3.3 revolutions per second, so that the motion can be read.
